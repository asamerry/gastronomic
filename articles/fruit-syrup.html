Overview

What are fruit syrups?

At their simplest level, fruit syrups are sweet, pourable liquids flavored with fruit. They can be made from fresh or frozen fruit, fruit juice, or other parts of the fruit that are generally combined with sugar to extract, preserve, and concentrate flavors. The resulting syrups can vary considerably in flavor and consistency. The fruit used, ratio of sugar to fruit, amount of added water, and use of heat all influence the finished product.

Origin and History

Fruit syrups do not have a single identifiable origin. Instead, they belong to a much broader history of preserving and concentrating fruits and their juices. Even before refined sugar became widely available, fruits could be cooked down into concentrated syrups or combined with sweeteners such as honey. In ancient Rome, for example, grape must was boiled down to produce syrups such as sapa, defrutum, and caroenum. These syrups were commonly used as sweeteners, flavorings, and coloring agents, and could be used to preserve fruits such as pears and quinces. As sugar became more accessible, it provided another way to preserve fruit and its flavor in preparations such as syrups, jams, jellies, and preserves.

Different forms of fruit syrup can be found across culinary traditions. In Korea, for example, fruits and other ingredients can be combined with sugar or honey to make cheong. Although these preparations vary considerably in their ingredients and techniques, they share the basic idea of capturing fruit's flavor in a concentrated, sweetened form.

How Fruit Syrups Are Made

There are many ways to make a fruit syrup. Cooking fruit with sugar and water softens the fruit and extracts its juices while simultaneously concentrating its flavor. Fruit can also be blended directly with simple syrup and strained, producing a syrup with a fresher flavor. Another approach is to combine fruit directly with sugar and allow it to rest. The sugar draws water and flavor compounds from the fruit, gradually creating a syrup without cooking. Each approach produces a somewhat different result, making the best method dependent on the fruit and the desired flavor.

Cheong

A similar heatless technique has an important place in Korean cuisine. Cheong (청) is a broad category of sweetened preparations that includes syrups and fruit preserves. Fruit cheong can be prepared by combining fruit with sugar and allowing the mixture to rest as the sugar draws liquid and flavor from the fruit. One example is maesil-cheong, which is made from green plums and sugar. Fruit cheong can be used to sweeten drinks and in desserts, sauces, and other dishes.

Variations

Fruit syrups can be varied by changing not only the fruit but also the sugar and additional flavorings. White sugar provides relatively little flavor of its own, allowing the fruit to remain prominent, while alternatives such as brown or raw sugar can contribute additional caramel and molasses flavors. An apple or pear brown sugar syrup, for example, can emphasize the warmer flavors associated with the cooked versions of these fruits.

Citrus syrups can also make use of the flavorful oils found in citrus peel. One traditional preparation, known as oleo saccharum, is made by covering citrus peels with sugar and allowing them to rest. The sugar extracts aromatic oils from the peel, producing an intensely citrus-flavored syrup that is quite different from one made primarily with citrus juice. Lemon is traditional, but oranges, grapefruits, and other citrus fruits can also be used.

Fruits can also be combined with herbs, spices, and other aromatic ingredients to produce more complex flavors. Mint, basil, rosemary, ginger, vanilla, lemongrass, and even chile peppers can complement different fruits. These ingredients can be incorporated during cooking or allowed to infuse into the syrups, depending on the desired flavor and preparation method.

Different fruits can also be combined in a single syrup. Berries can be mixed with one another, stone fruits can be paired together, and citrus can add acidity and aroma to sweeter fruits. These variations make the basic methods adaptable to both the ingredients available and the intended use of the finished syrup.

Recipes

For consistency, I've used 400 grams of fresh El Dorado plums (about 8-10 medium-sized plums) for each recipe, however you can experiment with different fruits, fresh or frozen, as well as combinations of fruits in single syrups.

Method 1: Heat

Ingredients

  • 400 g plums, pitted and quartered
  • 100 g sugar
  • 100 g water

Directions

  1. Add plums, sugar, and water to a saucepan, mix, and bring to a gentle simmer.
  2. Let simmer for about 10 minutes, stirring often. When the plums soften, mash them gently.
  3. Strain through a fine-mesh strainer to separate the syrup.

Notes

  1. Yield: approx. 250 grams
  2. This method produces the deepest, most concentrated fruit flavor while requiring the least added sugar.
  3. The concentrated flavor comes at the expense of yield. Since little water is added, the amount of syrup produced depends heavily on the water content of the fruit. Juicy fruits such as plums, strawberries, and peaches will generally produce more syrup than firmer fruits such as apples.
  4. The strained fruit can be saved and used as a topping for yogurt, ice cream, or pancakes, or you can blend it into a purée.

Method 2: Blend

Ingredients

  • 400 g plums, pitted and quartered
  • 400 g 1:1 simple syrup

Directions

  1. Blend the plums and simple syrup until combined thoroughly.
  2. Strain through a fine-mesh strainer to separate the syrup.

Notes

  1. Yield: approx. 650 grams
  2. This method produces a fresh, natural fruit flavor since the fruit is not cooked.
  3. Blending produces the largest yield of the three proposed methods, but added simple syrup dilutes the fruit and produces a less concentrated flavor than the heated method.
  4. This is the quickest of the three recipes.

Method 3: Wait

Ingredients

  • 400 g plums, pitted and quartered
  • 400 g sugar

Directions

  1. Mix plums and 200 grams of sugar in an air-tight container.
  2. Once thoroughly combined, pour the remaining 200 grams of sugar over the top of the plums. Try to cover the fruit completely.
  3. Leave in the fridge for at least a week. The mixture needs to remain cold to prevent fermentation.
  4. Once ready, strain through a fine-mesh strainer to separate the syrup.

Notes

  1. Yield: approx. 270 grams
  2. This method produces a concentrated fruit flavor without applying heat, preserving more of the fruit's fresh character than the heated method.
  3. Despite using the most sugar, this method produces relatively little syrup and requires considerably more time than the other methods. However, much of the sugar will not dissolve, so the amount of sugar added is greater than the amount actually incorporated into the finished syrup.
  4. This method is similar to preparations used for Korean fruit cheong, in which fruit and sugar are left together over time to produce a sweet syrup.
