Overview

What is kimchi?

Kimchi is a broad category of Korean foods made by salting and seasoning vegetables and allowing them to ferment. Napa cabbage is perhaps the most recognizable base, but kimchi can be made from many different vegetables, including radish, cucumber, and green onion. Seasonings vary considerably, but commonly include ingredients such as gochugaru (Korean red pepper flakes), garlic, ginger, green onion, and fermented seafood.

Kimchi is an important part of Korean cuisine and is commonly served as a banchan, or side dish, alongside a meal. It can also be used as an ingredient in dishes such as kimchi jjigae, fried rice, pancakes, noodles, and dumplings.

Origin and History

Kimchi developed from longstanding Korean methods of preserving vegetables with salt and fermentation. These preservation techniques allowed vegetables to be stored for periods when fresh produce was less readily available, particularly during the winter.

Kimchi has changed considerably throughout its history. Earlier forms would not have resembled the bright red kimchi that is familiar today. Chili peppers arrived in Korea only after contact with the Americas, and their eventual incorporation into kimchi contributed to the development of many modern varieties. Over time, different regions and households developed their own combinations of vegetables, seasonings, and fermentation techniques.

Variations

There are hundreds of varieties of kimchi, differing according to vegetable, region, season, and household. Baechu kimchi, made primarily from napa cabbage, is among the most familiar. Kkakdugi is made from cubed Korean radish, while oi sobagi consists of cucumbers stuffed with seasonings. Pa kimchi uses green onions, and gat kimchi is made from mustard greens.

Even within baechu kimchi, the cabbage can be prepared in different ways. Pogi kimchi is made by keeping napa cabbages in halves or quarters and spreading the seasoning mixture between their leaves. Mak kimchi, by comparison, is made by cutting the cabbage into smaller pieces before seasoning it. This makes mak kimchi somewhat quicker and easier to prepare, while producing a similar overall flavor. The term mak roughly conveys the idea of doing something casually or without fuss, reflecting the style's simpler preparation.

Not all kimchi is red or particularly spicy. Baek kimchi, or white kimchi, is prepared without gochugaru and generally has a lighter, milder flavor. Dongchimi is another distinctive variety in which radishes ferment in a substantial amount of brine, producing a refreshing kimchi whose liquid is consumed along with the vegetables.

Gimjang

Gimjang is the traditional practice of preparing and sharing large quantities of kimchi, particularly in late autumn in preparation for winter. Historically, making enough kimchi to last through the colder months requires considerable labor, encouraging families and communities to work together.

The practice extends beyond food preservation. Recipes, techniques, and preferences are passed between generations, while families and neighbors participate in preparing and sharing kimchi. In recognition of this cultural importance, UNESCO inscribed "Gimjang, making and sharing kimchi in the Republic of Korea" on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2013.

Uses

  • Serve as a side dish. Kimchi is commonly served as a banchan alongside rice and other side dishes. Its sour, spicy, and savory flavor provides a strong contrast to milder foods.
  • Make kimchi jjigae. Pork, tofu, onions, and other vegetables are added to a stew flavored with sour, well-fermented kimchi.
  • Try kimchi bokkeumbap. Chop kimchi and stir-fry with day-old rice. Top with a fried egg, green onions, or sesame seeds.
  • Kimchi jeon. Chopped kimchi and kimchi juice can be mixed into a simple batter and pan-fried to make savory kimchi pancakes.
  • Add to noodles. Kimchi can be served alongside or incorporated directly into noodle dishes. Its acidity and spice pair especially well with rich broths and sauces.
  • Use as a filling. Finely chopped kimchi can be mixed with tofu, pork, or other ingredients to make fillings for foods such as mandu.
  • Put it in a sandwich or wrap. Kimchi's acidity and crunch make it a useful substitute for pickles or other fermented condiments, especially with rich meats, eggs, or cheese.
  • Cook with the leftover juice. The liquid left in the container carries much of the kimchi's sour, spicy flavor and can be added to stews, fried rice, sauces, batters, and marinades rather than discarded.
